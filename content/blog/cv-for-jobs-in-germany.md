Most English-speaking employers in Germany are happy with an international CV. A few German habits still matter, though, and getting them right makes your application easier to say yes to.

## Photo, date of birth, marital status?

The classic German *Lebenslauf* had all three. **You don't need any of them.** The General Equal Treatment Act (AGG) protects applicants from discrimination based on age, gender, origin and more, so many employers now prefer CVs without these details — international teams especially. If you add one, use a professional headshot — never a holiday picture.

## Length and structure

- **One to two pages**, reverse chronological: most recent job first.
- For each role: title, company, city, dates (month and year), and 3–5 bullet points with **results**, not duties ("cut report time from 2 days to 2 hours").
- **Explain gaps** of more than a few months in one line (parental leave, relocation, studies). German recruiters notice unexplained gaps.
- Save as **PDF** and name it `Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf`.

## Say where you stand on the work permit

This is the line that saves you the most rejections. Put it under your contact details:

- *EU citizen — no work permit needed*, or
- *Residence permit for work (§18b), valid until 06/2028*, or
- *Eligible for the EU Blue Card — degree recognised (anabin H+)*.

Recruiters screen out unclear cases first. Make yours clear. Not sure about the Blue Card? Read [EU Blue Card 2026: salary thresholds](eu-blue-card-salary-2026.html).

## Languages: use the CEFR levels

Write *German — B1 (Goethe certificate, 2025)* rather than "intermediate". Employers in Germany read A1–C2 fluently. If you are learning, say so: *German — A2, attending evening course*. It shows commitment even for English-only roles.

## Cover letter, salary and start date

- Many startups no longer read cover letters; corporates still do. If the form asks for one, keep it to **half a page**: why this company, why you, what you'll do in the first months.
- *Gehaltsvorstellung* (salary expectation) is asked for often. Give a **gross yearly range** — check what similar jobs pay first.
- *Eintrittstermin* (start date) and your notice period: state them plainly, e.g. *available from 1 March, 4 weeks' notice*.

## References and certificates

German employees receive an *Arbeitszeugnis* (written reference) from every employer, and recruiters may ask for them. If you don't have any, a short reference letter or two contacts is fine — just be ready to explain that references work differently in your country.

## Before you hit send

1. Does the CV mention the tools and skills from the job ad, in the same words?
2. Is the work-permit line there?
3. Is it a PDF with your name in the file name?
